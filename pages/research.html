Ageing is the strongest risk factor for neurodegenerative disease, yet the molecular events that turn a healthy old brain into a diseased one remain poorly resolved. Senescent cells accumulate with age and secrete a complex mixture of proteins, lipids and extracellular vesicles (the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, SASP) that inflames tissue and disrupts neighbouring cells. Our laboratory uses quantitative mass-spectrometry proteomics as a common language to connect these processes across scales: single proteins and their modifications, the vesicles that carry them, the cells that release them, and the plasma of the people in whom we hope to detect and modify them.

Objectives

  1. Define the proteomic and lipidomic cargo of senescent-cell extracellular vesicles and establish which components are detectable in human plasma as markers of biological age.
  2. Map how senescent astrocytes, microglia and neurons remodel the ageing brain proteome and drive neuroinflammation relevant to Alzheimer's disease.
  3. Build human-relevant models — hiPSC-derived neural cells and 3D organoids — to test senolytic, senomorphic and vesicle-based interventions.
  4. Develop and share proteomics workflows (DIA, low-input, PTM and thermal-proteome profiling) and analysis tools that make these questions tractable for the wider community.

Research themes

A.

Cellular senescence, the SASP and extracellular vesicles

Senescent cells communicate with their environment through soluble factors and through extracellular vesicles (EVs) that package proteins, lipids and nucleic acids. Using DIA proteomics and lipidomics we have characterised the EV cargo released by senescent human cells and shown that circulating exosomes from older adults carry overlapping, age-associated signatures. We are now asking which of these cargo proteins are causal — altering recipient-cell behaviour — and which are simply informative as biomarkers.

B.

Healthy brain ageing, neuroinflammation and Alzheimer's disease

Glial senescence is an emerging driver of the chronic neuroinflammation that precedes neurodegeneration. We combine mouse brain proteomics across the lifespan with hiPSC-derived neurons, astrocytes and microglia to ask how senescent glia change the neuronal proteome, secretome and cell surface, and whether removing or reprogramming those cells restores homeostasis. A five-year programme on senescence-driven neuroinflammation in Alzheimer's disease underpins this theme.

C.

Proteomics technology development and data science

Many of the most interesting questions in ageing biology involve small, precious samples — sorted cell populations, micro-dissected tissue, vesicles. We develop low-input and single-cell proteomics methods, post-translational-modification and thermal-proteome profiling workflows, and reproducible quality-control pipelines on Orbitrap Exploris 480 and Fusion platforms. On the computational side we apply machine learning to integrate proteomic and metabolomic data at the pathway level.

D.

Translational biomarkers — from infectious disease to ageing

Our biomarker work began with clinical proteomics of severe vivax malaria and dengue in Indian cohorts, including a Raman-spectroscopy classifier that discriminates malaria- and dengue-infected sera. The same discipline — careful cohorts, orthogonal validation and honest statistics — now guides our search for plasma and EV-based markers of biological age, and our comparisons of affinity (SomaScan, Olink) and mass-spectrometry platforms for clinical proteomics.

Proteomics Facility

Sandip directs the Proteomics Facility of the MRC Toxicology Unit. The facility offers end-to-end support — experimental design, sample preparation, LC-MS/MS acquisition (DDA, DIA, TMT, label-free and targeted), reproducible quality control, and data analysis in Spectronaut, DIA-NN, MaxQuant and R — together with training for PhD students, postdoctoral researchers and external collaborators. Enquiries: proteomics@mrc-tox.cam.ac.uk.
