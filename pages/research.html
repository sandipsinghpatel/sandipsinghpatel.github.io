Ageing is the single strongest risk factor for neurodegenerative disease, yet we still cannot say with confidence which molecular events separate a healthy old brain from a diseased one. Our work starts from a specific suspect: the senescent cell. Cells that have permanently withdrawn from the cell cycle accumulate with age and, far from being inert, secrete a complex mixture of proteins, lipids and extracellular vesicles — the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, or SASP — that inflames the surrounding tissue and alters the behaviour of healthy neighbours.

Quantitative mass-spectrometry proteomics is the common language we use to follow that process across scales. The same measurement principle lets us ask what a single senescent cell secretes, what its vesicles carry, how a recipient neuron or glial cell responds, and whether any of it can be detected in a tube of human plasma. Working across those scales in one laboratory is the point: a mechanism that cannot be measured in people remains a hypothesis, and a blood marker without a mechanism remains a correlation.

Objectives

  1. Define the proteomic and lipidomic cargo of senescent-cell extracellular vesicles, and establish which components are detectable in human plasma as markers of biological age.
  2. Map how senescent astrocytes, microglia and neurons remodel the ageing brain proteome, and identify the crosstalk that drives chronic neuroinflammation in Alzheimer's disease.
  3. Build human-relevant models — hiPSC-derived neural cells and 3D organoids — in which senolytic, senomorphic and vesicle-based interventions can be tested with cell-type resolution.
  4. Develop and share the proteomics workflows this demands: data-independent acquisition, low-input and single-cell methods, surface-omics, secretomics and thermal-proteome profiling.

Research themes

A.

Cellular senescence, the SASP and extracellular vesicles

Senescent cells speak to their environment through soluble factors and through extracellular vesicles (EVs) that package proteins, lipids and nucleic acids. Using data-independent-acquisition proteomics together with lipidomics, we characterised the vesicles released by senescent human cells and compared them with circulating exosomes isolated from the plasma of younger and older adults. A shared, age-associated signature emerged across the two — evidence that vesicle cargo is both a route by which senescence propagates and an accessible readout of it.

The open questions are causal ones. Which cargo proteins actually change recipient-cell behaviour, and which are merely along for the ride? Does the vesicle surface determine which cells receive the message? We approach these with engineered vesicles, cargo depletion, and surface-omics of both donor and recipient populations.

B.

Healthy brain ageing, neuroinflammation and Alzheimer's disease

Glial senescence is emerging as a driver of the chronic, low-grade neuroinflammation that precedes overt neurodegeneration. Our five-year programme on senescence-driven neuroinflammation centres on microglia–astrocyte crosstalk: which cell type senesces first, what each secretes, and how that exchange tips a resilient brain into a vulnerable one.

The experimental strategy is deliberately cell-type-resolved. We combine mouse brain proteomics across the lifespan with human iPSC-derived neurons, astrocytes and microglia, profiling the proteome, the secretome and the cell surface of each population separately rather than in bulk. Reporter-guided screening then lets us ask whether senolytic or senomorphic compounds, or targeted recombinant small-EV delivery, can restore homeostasis in the cells that matter.

C.

Proteomics technology and data science

The most interesting questions in ageing biology tend to involve the least material: sorted glial populations, micro-dissected tissue, vesicles from a few millilitres of plasma. Much of our methodological work is therefore about sensitivity and reproducibility at low input — DIA methods on an Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid with FAIMS Pro, careful quality control across long acquisition series, and workflows for secretomics, surface-omics, post-translational-modification profiling and thermal-proteome profiling.

On the computational side we integrate proteomic and metabolomic data at the pathway level and use machine learning where it earns its place — for feature selection and pattern recognition across modalities, rather than as a substitute for experimental validation.

D.

Translational biomarkers — from infectious disease to ageing

Our biomarker work began in clinical proteomics of severe Plasmodium vivax malaria and dengue, in collaboration with hospitals across India, and included a Raman-spectroscopy classifier able to discriminate malaria- and dengue-infected sera. That work established the discipline we still apply: well-characterised cohorts, orthogonal validation, and statistics that survive scrutiny.

The same standards now guide our search for plasma and EV-based markers of biological age, and our comparisons of affinity-based platforms with mass spectrometry for clinical proteomics — a contextual assessment of what each technology can and cannot deliver, rather than a ranking.

How we work

1.

Measure at cell-type resolution

Bulk tissue averages away the biology we care about. Wherever possible we separate populations first and accept the harder, lower-input measurement that follows.

2.

Pair mechanism with a measurable

Every mechanistic project carries a question about what could be detected in an accessible sample — plasma, CSF, conditioned medium — so the finding has somewhere to go.

3.

Validate orthogonally

Discovery proteomics generates candidates, not conclusions. Targeted assays, imaging and functional readouts decide which survive.

4.

Share the method

Protocols, acquisition parameters and analysis code are written to be reused, and raw data are deposited on publication.

Proteomics Facility

Sandip directs the Proteomics Facility of the MRC Toxicology Unit, which supports researchers across the Unit, the University and external institutions. The facility covers experimental design, sample preparation, LC-MS/MS acquisition (DDA, DIA, TMT, label-free and targeted), reproducible quality control, and data analysis, together with hands-on training. If you are weighing up a proteomics experiment, we are happy to advise on feasibility, minimum sample requirements, quantification strategy, approximate cost and realistic turnaround before you commit any material.
